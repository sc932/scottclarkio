The standard advice to a second-time founder is: learn from your mistakes. I took it seriously. When I started Distributional in 2023, I carried a well-studied list of everything I'd gotten wrong at my first company, SigOpt, and I was determined not to repeat any of it.

I mostly didn't. The mistakes that hurt were new ones, and they came from the place I wasn't watching. I learned from my SigOpt mistakes so I could make all-new mistakes faster - but I overfit to my old successes and turned *them* into mistakes.

## What worked the first time

On paper, SigOpt was the run you'd want. We went through Y Combinator in 2015, raised from investors we admired, took the Bayesian optimization tooling I'd built in grad school and at Yelp and turned it into an enterprise product, grew the team, stood up a sales motion, and sold the company to Intel in 2020. Plenty went wrong along the way. But the big timing calls worked: when we hired, when we scaled, when we leaned into go-to-market, when we sold.

And so those calls stopped feeling like calls. They hardened into judgment, which is the polite word for priors you've stopped questioning. Hiring ahead of product-market fit had worked, because the ML-infrastructure market grew into the capacity we built. Standing up sales early had worked, because buyers were already forming budgets around our category. Eight years later, none of that felt like a playbook I was choosing to re-run. It felt like knowing what I was doing.

## Success is a worse teacher than failure

Mistakes announce themselves. You miss the quarter, you lose the deal, you watch a launch land flat, and the lesson arrives with a timestamp and a bruise. Failure hands you a gradient: painful, specific, directional.

Success hands you a reinforced prior and almost no information about why it worked. Was it the play? The timing? The market regime? Luck? One company outcome can't separate those variables. A founding career is a training set with one sample per company, and I fit a confident model to mine. I have a PhD in applied math. Overfitting is roughly the first danger you learn to fear, and I did it anyway, with my own resume as the training data.

It gets worse, because the environment isn't stationary. The AI market between 2015 and 2023 didn't drift, it jumped: model capabilities jumping several times a year, categories forming and dissolving in months, buyers rethinking budgets mid-cycle. It's not that my old plays were wrong in some universal sense; it's that they were fit to a distribution that no longer existed. Success also tilts you all the way toward exploitation. You've found high ground before, so you march straight at where it was. When the terrain moves, the value of exploration goes back up, and your own experience is the voice insisting you don't need it.

## Winners fight the last war too

The military version is a cliche: generals always fight the last war. The part that gets left off is that the winners do it hardest. France came out of World War I on the winning side holding a lesson written in blood: prepared defense held, offense bled you dry. So they spent a decade building the perfect answer to 1916. The Maginot Line largely worked as engineered; almost none of it fell to assault. What failed was the assumption underneath it, about where and how the next war would arrive: the German attack came through the Ardennes, terrain the planners had rated close to impassable, and the Battle of France was over in about six weeks.

The Maginot Line gets remembered as stupidity. It wasn't. It was a well-learned lesson from the previous environment, applied faithfully to a new one. Losing pushes you to change; winning pushes you to double down. That asymmetry is what makes success the more dangerous teacher.

## What re-running the playbook looked like

I hired and scaled ahead of product-market fit, because last time that timing had been right. This mistake compounds quietly, step by step. You hire ahead of fit, and the new team needs a roadmap. A roadmap demands conviction you don't actually have yet, so you commit to your best current guess. Once the org is executing on that guess, changing it costs a reorg, so each piece of contrary evidence gets discounted a little. Momentum starts being about the machine instead of the market. From inside, none of this looks like a mistake. It looks like execution.

I built out the go-to-market motion before the pull was organic, because last time the champions had shown up early and met us halfway. This time we were pushing, and push is expensive in exactly the places pull is free.

## Naming the quadrant

And we had vision-market fit more than once. Smart, senior people agreed the problem was real and coming: AI systems need testing you can trust. Rooms leaned in. Pilots started. When we repositioned the product, the rooms leaned in again. Every signal a founder wants to see, except the one that pays.

It took me too long to treat that pattern as a distinct false positive with a name. So, definitions, because the names turned out to be the lesson.

*Vision-market fit* is resonance: people with real budgets and real problems agree that what you see coming is coming, and interest shows up wherever you push. The tell is what never happens next. The interest stays proportional to your pushing. When you stop, it stops.

*Product-market fit* is pull: buyers you didn't call show up, budgets form around your category without you in the room, and customers put you in their architecture diagram without being asked. You stop pushing and it keeps moving.

From inside any single good meeting, the two are indistinguishable. Vision-market fit is dangerous precisely because it looks like the opening scenes of product-market fit. The two are different, and scaling on top of the first one means scaling a hypothesis. The difference only shows up in what happens when you stop pushing, which is exactly the experiment a scaling company never runs.

Naming the quadrant is what makes it escapable. An unnamed false positive just feels like early traction running a little late. A named one is checkable: you can ask, every quarter, what moved this quarter that we didn't push, and write the answer down.

In our case, the answer had been on the page for a while: the doubts that eventually proved decisive were sitting in the margins of our own strategy decks, in our own words, well before I acted on them. Strategy documents archive your disconfirming evidence whether you mean them to or not; reread your margins. And there's an irony worth saying plainly: I spent those years building software to catch the gap between how people assumed their AI systems behaved and how they actually behaved. The assumption I tested least was the one my own company was standing on. Reading the evidence late is on me. Whether or not the AI-testing market matures the way I believed it would, and I still think a version of it will, it wasn't maturing on the clock we were running.

Which brings up the third definition, the one the name buys you. *The honest process* is what you run when the answer keeps coming back "nothing pulled": you test the company against the real market instead of against your own hopes. Real conversations, serious diligence, real alternatives on the table. Not because you've already decided to sell or to stop, but because a choice made against real alternatives is a decision, and a choice made against imagined ones is a story you tell yourself.

## The accounting

So here's the honest version, without spin in either direction. Distributional did real work on a real problem, with customers and investors I'm grateful for, and the plays I imported didn't convert vision into product-market fit. Those were my calls.

When the evidence became hard to argue with, we ran the process instead of arguing with it, all the way to the end: term sheets on the table, an offer for the whole team. At the end of the day, that's what made what came next a decision instead of a drift.

We chose a hard pivot over a slow fade. We cleaned it up nicely: commitments honored, most of the team landing well together, and the company coming out the other side pointed at something I believe in even more.

That something is Talaria Scientific, and today's announcement carries the details ([Distributional is now Talaria Scientific](https://distributional.com/blog/distributional-is-now-talaria)). The short version of the mission: helping computational scientists go faster.

## New mistakes, on purpose

What I'm doing differently is not "avoiding mistakes." The goal isn't fewer mistakes; it's newer ones, made faster, against current evidence instead of old wins. Three changes, which are really one change seen from three angles:

Staying lean. Talaria today is a team of one plus contractors. Not because scale is bad, but because scale buys speed at the price of rigidity, and rigidity is what slowed my updating last time. Headcount converts hypotheses into commitments. I want my hypotheses cheap to kill for as long as possible.

Being my own customer. This time the ideal user is me. I spend my days running computational research through the product I'm building, and when it's slow or wrong I feel it the same afternoon. The feedback loop went from quarters to hours, which means my gradient information finally comes from the present instead of from 2016. Being my own user shortens the loop; it doesn't prove demand. External researchers get the deciding vote on that.

Sequencing truth before scale. Vision-market fit opens doors, and I'm glad for every one, but it isn't the signal to hire against. Product truth first, then pull, then scale, in that order, with the order enforced by keeping the company too small to fake it.

That being said, the answer isn't to invert every play that worked before. Inverting is still letting the old data drive; it's overfitting with a minus sign in front. Some SigOpt lessons transfer just fine. The discipline is holding the old playbook as a prior, weighting it by how much the world has moved, and letting present evidence make the calls. In a market moving this fast, the honest weight on eight-year-old experience is smaller than feels fair.

There's a bigger question underneath all of this, about when to keep climbing the mountain you're on and when to change mountains entirely. Careers have many paths up many mountains, and that idea deserves its own post.

For now, the all-new mistakes are underway. I can't tell you which ones they are yet, which is the point: if I could name them, they'd be the old kind. The new map is mostly blank, but one quadrant on it is already named. In a few years Talaria will have its own list of what worked, and this time I know which list is the dangerous one.
