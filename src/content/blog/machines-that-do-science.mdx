On September 15, 2026, I sat on a panel called Machines That Do Science at Imagination in Action's Silicon Valley AI Summit, on the Frontier Stage at Google Bay View. Frazer Anderson of Link Ventures moderated, and Nick Edwards of Potato and Piotr Byrski of Molecule.one had the other two chairs. The question on the slide behind us: AI is now proposing molecules, running experiments and drawing conclusions on its own. How much of the loop closes without a person in it, and how do you know when to trust the result? My argument: the mechanical middle of science is now in distribution for these models, the out-of-distribution question still belongs to the scientist, and a system that keeps the record is what makes the faster loop trustworthy. Imagination in Action recorded the session. The video is below, then the summary, then the conversation question by question.

## The summary

Frazer opened with the strongest version of the question everybody in this field gets asked: should scientists just be providing training data to the frontier labs, and once we have fully general intelligence, let the system go solve the science? I answered with the two ideas I use to think about it. The Bitter Lesson says more data and more compute solve the problem. The No Free Lunch theorems say a specialized model with better prior knowledge can beat a general-purpose one. Both are right, and between them there is always a frontier. The models today are very good at in-distribution work, and a lot of the scientific process is in distribution: spinning up heterogeneous compute, replicating a baseline, doing the ETL on a ton of data. The next question is out of distribution, and surfing that frontier is where the exciting work and the abundance come from.

Nick and Piotr close their loops in physical labs, Nick in biology and Piotr in chemistry, and both answered from the lab side: the feedback cycles are long, much of the literature does not reproduce, and a physical lab is a tool an agent can call, with the real world as the final validation. I think of physics as a distillation attack on the universe. The job is finding the right fidelity for the experiment, in silico with a physics-informed neural network, then in the lab, and every rung on that ladder has its own confidence interval and its own price tag. You go up and down it as far as the question needs, and a world model is not going to collapse the ladder any time soon.

Where does the loop get stuck? Piotr's answer was the trade-offs: his lab tests unpurified compounds, accepts the noisier data, and gets a hundred times as many measurements. Nick's was utilization: labs have spent a decade buying robots that sit in a corner collecting dust, because automating one experiment takes weeks to months of human work up front, and good design of experiments is the way around that. I spent seven years on that problem at SigOpt, which was Bayesian optimization as a service, and the trade-off I kept seeing was exploration versus exploitation: many weak estimators can be the better way to explore the space, and then you exploit with the expensive system. The bottleneck underneath all of it is where you point the optimizer. I said the line I have been saying for a decade: "The best thing about a black-box optimizer is that it will optimize anything. The worst thing about a black-box optimizer is that it will blindly optimize anything." If you get somewhere fast and it is the wrong place, that is the same as not getting there.

Piotr called the upside engineered serendipity: the history of science is paved with luck, and a lab that runs enough experiments can engineer its own. Frazer compressed that to surface area, which is how I think about the systems I build. They are what-if machines. I can do in six hours what took me six months in my PhD, which means I ask more questions, read more papers, and run the experiment reviewer 2 asked for instead of explaining it away. Spin the flywheel faster and you expose more surface area, and you end up climbing mountains you did not know you wanted to climb.

The trust question came in two forms. The first was sycophancy: these models can be prompted into supporting almost any conclusion, so what do you do internally to safeguard hypothesis generation? The analogy I use is whether you are building a mech suit or an android, and I have landed on the mech suit: a tool that lets the scientist do things better, faster, and more efficiently, with the scientist still directing it, because the combinatorial space of hypotheses is too large and the models want to find the median. Ask one for an architecture and it suggests a softmax, because a softmax is everywhere in the literature. The scientist supplies the taste and decides what to ask next. Maybe GPT-15 or 16 replaces us all, but for now the out-of-distribution search is still human driven. Nick's answer came from Karl Popper: hypotheses formulated so they can be falsified, and the rigor is what frees the scientist to be creative.

The second form was fraud. Nick calls it hypotheslop, and my short answer was that these are massive p-hacking machines. My longer answer is what these systems are good at. One is the interdisciplinary connection, the move that let applied mathematicians publish across fields. The other is note-taking: keeping track of everything you tried, and in silico that means bit-exact reproducible results with every pseudo-random seed pinned, so you can hand someone a Docker container and they reproduce the result exactly, instead of academic code where the hyperparameters you tuned are left as an exercise for the reader. That leads to more trustworthy and more verifiable science, if we hold people to a bar they did not have before. I have plenty of pre-AI academic slop code of my own out there, and there is no excuse for that anymore.

Frazer's last question was what is missing from the stack. Everybody says it, but my answer is more compute, and then more real-world validation, which is what Nick and Piotr do. Nick wants drivers for scientific equipment and some convergence on standards. Piotr wants equipment, equipment, equipment, because even the instruments built for automated labs were built for human-led workflows, and they break when you run a hundred times as many experiments on them. Frazer refused all three answers. The correct one was more venture capitalists.

## The conversation, question by question

Frazer asked five questions. Under each one is what I said, lightly cleaned up from Imagination in Action's recording, with what Nick and Piotr said beside it.

### Introductions

Frazer opened by asking each of us how we are spending our time in this AI moment.

Happy to be here. My name is Scott Clark. I'm building [Talaria Scientific](https://talariasci.com), tools to help physicists and applied mathematicians do better in silico research. So think spinning up environments, getting baselines, having better reproducible research. Kind of like a Cursor for computational science.

Nick introduced Potato, "AI-native infrastructure for autonomous labs," which he described as CAD for biological experiments: take an experiment, suggest how to optimize it, formalize it, compile it into code that runs on an instrument, and learn from the result. Piotr introduced Molecule.one's Maria, "a physical AI system that is capable of doing autonomous research in chemistry": a set of agents and specialized models that cover the workflow from literature research through experiment design to data analysis, wired to a purpose-built lab where the experiments run and the real world reports back.

### Why can't the frontier labs just solve all of the science?

There is a school of thought, Frazer said, that scientists should all be working for the frontier labs in some sense: provide the training data, wait for fully general intelligence, and let the system go solve the science. Why is that not consistent with the fabric of reality? He started with me.

I think there's this tension between two concepts. There's the Bitter Lesson, from Richard Sutton, which says that more data will just solve all problems: you don't need to do feature engineering for natural language processing or computer vision anymore. But then there's also the No Free Lunch theorem, that a specialized model that has better prior knowledge can beat a general-purpose model. And I think there's always going to be this frontier. The models today are very good at doing a lot of in-distribution work, and it turns out a lot of the scientific process is in distribution: spinning up heterogeneous compute, replicating a baseline, doing the ETL on a ton of data. But there's always going to be this frontier of what's next, what's the thing that's out of distribution. So I still think there's a lot to be done for scientists, and ultimately surfing that frontier is where all the real exciting stuff and abundance comes from.

Nick's answer was about data. Math is reaching a level of superintelligence, he said, because you can immediately verify an infinite number of hypotheses, and biology cannot, because the feedback cycles are incredibly long: much of the literature is not reproducible, the data labs do have is not tied to provenance, and a new, complete set of data has to be built, across so many modalities that it is not getting centralized any time soon. Piotr's was about interfaces: "a physical lab is a tool that an agent can call." Before you run the experiment you do not know the outcome, so the real world provides the final validation, and the place for teams outside the frontier labs is building the interfaces between the real world and the intelligence layer the frontier companies are best at.

I love that. And fundamentally physics is just a distillation attack on the universe. So it's about finding the right fidelity for your experimentation. Some of that can be done in silico with a physics-informed neural network, but then you definitely need to take it in vitro or in vivo to make sure that it actually holds up. But even then, different experiments have different confidence intervals, so you can always chase this a little bit further, to whatever level of accuracy or fidelity you absolutely need. It's always this ladder that you need to go up and down. And I don't think an in-distribution model, like a world model, is going to be able to solve all of that anytime soon.

Those confidence levels come with different price tags, Piotr added, and the trade-offs people have built an intuition for in human-led workflows are completely different for agentic science. His lab accepts data that is a bit noisier than a traditional lab's in return for two orders of magnitude more experimental capability.

### Where is the loop getting stuck?

Frazer wanted it concrete: the trade-offs that are different for agents, as specific as possible, and where things get stuck inside an autonomous lab today, robots knocking over glassware included.

Piotr's first example was purity. For years medicinal chemists at pharma and biotech companies held that every compound you test has to be 90 or 95 percent pure, and when you dig into why, the answer is that it has always been done that way. Purification is a large source of cost and delay, so his lab tests unpurified compounds: noisier data, more false positives and false negatives, and a hundred times as many measurements, which more than makes up for it. His second example was the lab itself. Four years ago, before agentic science was a phrase, the advice was to build AI that adapts to the current lab. They built a lab for the AI, and he thinks time has proven them right: adapt the experiments to what the computational side can do.

Nick's bottleneck is utilization. Labs have spent a decade on robotics and automation, and the instruments sit in a corner collecting dust, because getting a single experiment automated takes weeks to months of human work up front: reading papers to see which methods compare, isolating one factor at a time, iterating, then handing it to an engineer to script for the instrument. The way around it is very good design of experiments, mapping every parameter you could change and every condition you could test, with AI doing that upfront design and the translation to the robot, in tools humans can use now and agents can use next.

I 100 percent agree with both of those. My first startup, SigOpt, was Bayesian optimization as a service. You can think of it as design of experiments to make more efficient searching of these high-dimensional spaces. I ran that for seven years before selling it to Intel.

Frazer cut in: "There's no need to show off."

Fundamentally, we saw a lot of these same trade-offs. You have exploration versus exploitation, and it turns out many weak estimators can actually be better in that exploration phase, to learn as much as you can about the underlying space and model, and then you exploit with your very expensive systems. We saw this with the thousands of different research labs and scientists that we worked with over those seven years. But fundamentally, one of the biggest bottlenecks is: where do you even point a system like this? A joke we always used to say is that the best thing about a black-box optimizer is it'll optimize anything, and the worst thing about a black-box optimizer is it will blindly optimize anything. So making sure that you know exactly where on that Pareto frontier you want to find those trade-offs, or what your ultimate goal is, matters a lot, because if you can get there very fast but you get to the wrong place, it's the same as not getting there.

Piotr agreed, and added the most exciting development he has seen this year: models gaining the ability to pick the right problem to work on, beyond optimizing against the one they are handed. He calls the upside engineered serendipity. The history of science is paved with luck, penicillin being the famous case, and a machine-led lab can engineer its own luck because it can run and analyze so many experiments: "there's just no way that there's no scientific knowledge resting in all that data." Frazer's compression: luck is a matter of how much surface area you can expose.

Exactly. I like to think of some of these systems as what-if machines. Nowadays I can do something in six hours that used to take me six months in my PhD, which means I can ask more questions, I can read more papers, I can do things that otherwise would have been, oh yeah, I'm just going to explain this away, I don't want to deal with what reviewer 2 is telling me. But ultimately, by doing more of these experiments, by having that flywheel spin faster, we get a better surface area, and we end up climbing mountains that we didn't even know we wanted to climb.

### How do you keep a sycophantic model from supporting any conclusion?

We are entering a golden age of science, Frazer said, and also a golden age of bro science: sycophantic models can be prompted or encouraged into supporting almost any conclusion, and models ingesting more and more of their own slop could see performance decay right when our confidence in them peaks. What do you do internally to safeguard hypothesis generation: prompt tuning, fine-tuning your own models?

The analogy I like to think of is: are you building a mech suit, or are you building an android? At least the way that I've landed on this is building a mech suit that allows the scientist to do things better, faster, and more efficiently. But ultimately the scientist still needs to direct it, because the combinatorial space of all possible hypotheses is just too large, and sometimes these models want to find the median. So they'll suggest, oh, let's just throw a softmax here, because of course that's everywhere in the literature, when in reality you want something completely different. And so ultimately I think the scientist still needs to apply the taste, or whatever you want to call it, to what it is that you're exploring, and then decide what to ask next. The fully autonomous, maybe we'll get there. Maybe GPT-15 or 16 will fully replace us all. But for now I think that frontier, that out-of-distribution search, still needs to be human driven.

Nick went back to the philosophy of science he read during his PhD, Karl Popper in particular. Science is falsifiable, so formulate hypotheses so they can be explicitly tested, quantify what you expect going into an experiment, the quality of data that should come out and whether you reached it, and make predictions for the next step. The rigor and the formal structure, he said, are what let the scientist be more creative and use the skills the AIs are not as good at.

### Is there more scientific fraud now?

Frazer sees more fraud in the market today than he has ever seen, and asked whether we are seeing more scientific fraud. Nick's name for it: hypotheslop.

Yeah, they're massive p-hacking machines, for sure.

Piotr's answer was that chemistry is lucky here, because the verification is not that hard once you have a single hypothesis at the end of the process. The project his team ran with OpenAI found a real improvement to an important process in medicinal chemistry, and the output is a recipe any scientist, human or machine, can reproduce in their lab in about a day. Frazer asked whether the idea came out of a reasoning trace nobody else had, or was a throwaway suggestion, a hallucination that happened to be right. OpenAI published the chain of thought, Piotr said. GPT came up with the hypothesis but thought it was unlikely to work because of literature it found, and the project's budget of 40,000 experiments meant it got tested anyway, which is the engineered serendipity. The idea itself came from what his team calls cross-pollination: the model read papers from two domains, saw copper used with a particular reagent in one reaction, and wondered whether it would work in another. That is where machines improve on human-led science, he said, because we cannot read all the papers, let alone make the connections, which scale with the square of their number.

Nick's worry is the enshittification of science. The literature cannot be trusted a lot of the time, so results still have to be verified experimentally, and that is why he wants autonomous labs: to test and falsify the ideas people put online. Frazer's consolation was that it has already happened to culture, so not to worry.

I think there's two things that these systems are incredibly good at. One is finding these interdisciplinary connections. This was always like the applied physicist or the applied mathematician got to publish a lot, because they could speak a bunch of different fields and pull things together. Now the AIs can do that a lot for you. The other thing they're really good at is note-taking for you, and making sure that they keep track of everything that you tried. And especially in silico, that means you can create reproducible results that are bit exact, that can pin every single pseudo-random number generator seed throughout the entire system, and you can literally hand someone a Docker container and say, you can reproduce this result exactly, as opposed to, here's a bunch of academic code that's left as the exercise to the reader, oh whoops, I forgot that I tuned hyperparameters or something like that. Ultimately, I think that can lead to more trustworthy and more verifiable science. We just have to hold people to the bar that they wouldn't have had before. When I was a grad student, yeah, I tried to write down as much as I could, but I've got a lot of academic slop code, pre-AI slop, out there as well. There's no excuse for doing that anymore.

### What is missing from the stack?

Frazer's last question: what is missing from the stack, what has to be built to take this to the next level?

Everybody says it, but just more compute. A lot of the tools are there. There's a lot of great open source. It's easier than ever to build things, but we need more compute, and then more real-world validation, which is what these two guys do.

Science is unlocked by tools, Nick said, and for physical experimentation the missing tool is drivers: incredible scientific equipment that is hard to interface with. Anthropic has been doing interesting work there, and he hopes for convergence on standards. Piotr's answer was equipment, equipment, equipment. Even the instruments built in theory for automated labs are in fact built for human-led workflows, and when you try to run a hundred times as many experiments on them they break down. His team is talking to vendors about it, and he thinks lab hardware, a hard market, could have a renaissance if the vendors figure it out.

Frazer could not accept any of our answers. The correct one was more venture capitalists.
