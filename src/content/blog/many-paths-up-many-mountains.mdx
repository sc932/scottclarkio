Most career advice assumes there is one mountain. One summit that counts as making it, one right route up it, and a single line you're either ahead of or behind. Fall off the route and you failed. Get passed and you're losing. Take a job that isn't a promotion and you slipped down the slope everyone else is climbing.

I've been given that advice, and I've given it. It's wrong in a specific, math-shaped way. I spent my PhD and most of my career on optimization: finding the best point in a space you can't see all at once. The single-mountain picture is a poor model for the interesting search problems, and for careers especially. It's not many paths up one mountain. It's **many paths up many mountains**.

Three definitions first.

A **real option** is the right, but not the obligation, to make a move later. You pay a small premium to keep the choice alive, and you exercise it only if the world moves your way. The refundable deposit, not the wedding. Careers are full of these: the collaborator you still have coffee with in a field you left, the skill you keep current though your job stopped asking for it, the reputation that opens a door years after you last knocked.

A **base camp**, the way I'll use it here, is not a place you rest. It's anything you build once that more than one route can launch from: skills that transfer, people who would climb with you again, a track record that arrives before you do. Routes are exclusive; base camps are shared.

The **moving map** is the fact that the range itself doesn't hold still. Mountains here are fields, industries, technologies, and they rise and sink on decade timescales, sometimes faster. A survey from five years ago is a historical document, not a plan.

## The map moves

Start with the objective. In a textbook problem, someone hands you the function to optimize: minimize this loss, maximize that reward. A career is stranger. Nobody hands you the objective. You're the optimizer and also the one writing the function, and you're allowed to rewrite it. What "the top" means at twenty-two (money, or getting hired at all) isn't what it means at thirty-five (a problem worth a decade) or at fifty (who you got to do it with). The objective is non-stationary: it deforms under you as you age, as your family grows, as the world moves. That's why the altitude axis in the figure is labeled the way it is. Tracking a moving target isn't flakiness. It's what online optimization is for.

And it's not just the objective. The terrain moves too. The job I built the first stretch of my career on, applied optimization at a consumer internet company, barely existed as a job when I was picking graduate schools; the peaks I ended up climbing weren't on the map I started with. It cuts the other way as well: I've aimed a company at a summit I had surveyed carefully and watched the market under it shift while we climbed. I've been saying a version of this about optimization problems since 2014: you're not just climbing a mountain, you're surfing a wave. A career is the same sentence in slow motion. The fix is unglamorous: re-survey. Every so often, look up from the climb and ask which peaks still exist, which got taller, and what rose behind you while you were watching your feet.

## More than one peak is real

The surface isn't a single hill, either. It's multi-modal: many peaks, and more than one of them is real high ground. Founding a company, running a large org inside a big one, doing research, teaching, investing, building a home life that works: these are different peaks, and several stand as high as any other. There's no universal scalar that ranks them for everyone: each of us chooses the weighting, and the weighting changes. Anyone who tells you the startup peak dominates the teaching peak isn't measuring. They're standing on one and pointing.

I've stood on a few, and the honest version is that I couldn't see any of them from more than one mountain back. I came to Cornell planning a PhD in computational fluid dynamics and left one summer at Los Alamos pointed at bioinformatics instead. From campus I couldn't see Yelp. From inside Yelp I couldn't see SigOpt: it took open-sourcing an optimizer there (MOE) and watching teams who wanted it struggle to stand it up before "turn this into a company" was visible terrain. From SigOpt I couldn't see the Intel acquisition, or that I'd end up running a couple hundred people there as a VP. And from Intel I couldn't see Distributional, or the hard pivot I'm in the middle of right now, rebuilding it into Talaria Scientific and pointing it at computational science ([Distributional is now Talaria Scientific](https://distributional.com/blog/distributional-is-now-talaria)). Every summit revealed a range I couldn't have drawn from the valley below it. That isn't foresight; I had none worth bragging about. It's ridge geometry: you can't see past a ridge you haven't climbed, and every summit doubles as the survey point for the next range.

## The dip is information, not a verdict

Here's the part the single-mountain story gets most wrong: real ascents take you downhill along the way. Real terrain is rugged, and the way from a lower peak to a higher one runs through a saddle. You lose altitude to gain it. Any mountaineer knows a ridgeline is a run of ups and downs: you down-climb into the col before the next pitch, and giving up altitude there isn't a wrong turn. It's the route.

So when a stretch of your career feels like descent, there are two very different readings. One: you're in the saddle between where you were and somewhere higher, and the dip is transit. Two: the whole massif tops out low, no route from here reaches the height you want, and the dip is a verdict. Same feeling, opposite response: keep going, or switch mountains.

The part that actually helps: in one or two dimensions, a trap is usually a real local peak and you're stuck on it. But a career has many axes: money, craft, meaning, freedom, the people around you, what you're learning. In 2014, Dauphin and coauthors argued that in high-dimensional nonconvex landscapes, the traps that matter are mostly saddle points rather than true local optima: high along the axes you're staring at, with at least one direction that still climbs. The result doesn't port to careers as math, but it ports as a habit of looking: you feel stuck because you're staring down the axes that fall away. If the stuck place really is a saddle, an improving direction exists, and the work is figuring out whether that description fits.

And the dip compounds in a way that fools you. You take a role that reads as lateral, which knocks your title, which dents your confidence, which makes the next six ordinary-hard months feel like proof you blew the call, which tempts you to bail right when you're halfway up the far side of the saddle. The feeling is real. It just isn't data about the mountain.

## Switching mountains, and what you keep

Switching mountains, then, is a move, not a failure, and it has an honest price. In optimization we do it on purpose. A random restart drops the search somewhere new to find a different peak. Simulated annealing and basin-hopping build the jump into the method: run hot early and you'll accept moves that lose altitude, which is how you get off a peak that isn't the tallest; cool down and you commit. The restart isn't the algorithm giving up. It's how the search avoids settling for the first decent answer.

But restarts cost. When you jump, you give up your current position: the altitude, the momentum, the compounding. What you keep is your best-so-far and your prior, warm-starting the next basin with what you learned. In a career, that warm start is the base camp: the skills that transfer, the people who would climb with you again, the reputation that shows up before you do. I lost title and org and a lot of comfort each time I switched. I kept how to build and I kept the people, and nearly everything that made the next climb faster came out of those two camps. That's why the figure draws the camps at the junctions. A restart from a good base camp doesn't start at sea level.

That being said, restarts are also the most common way to fool yourself. If you jump every time you hit a saddle, you never descend far enough into any basin to learn how deep it was. That isn't exploration; it's noise wearing exploration's jacket. The tell is what the move is for. Real exploration moves toward a specific uncertainty whose answer could change what you do next; the information is worth the altitude you trade for it. Flight moves away from a feeling, because the climb got hard. Same jump, opposite reason, and only you can see which. It's not the switching that's the problem. It's switching to dodge the dip.

## Keep several paths warm

All of which lands on a strategy that sounds almost too plain: hold a portfolio of live paths, and walk one. A live path is a real option in the sense defined up top, and keeping it alive is mostly base-camp maintenance. Answer the conference invite. Review the paper. Give the guest lecture. Ship the small fix to the tool you'd use if you ever went back. Each deposit is small, and each one keeps open a route that silence would close. The paths marked "kept warm" in the figure aren't being climbed. They're being paid for, which is different, and much cheaper.

Options have a property that feels backwards until it doesn't: they're worth more when the world is less predictable. Volatility is what you're paying for. If the range held still, options would be wasted premium: survey once, pick the tallest peak you care about, and climb for forty years. It's because the map moves that the deposits pay. The same drift that erases the summit you planned for is the drift that raises new peaks, and a kept-warm path is how you reach one while others are still at the trailhead.

And here I have to argue against the comfortable reading of my own metaphor. A portfolio of paths is not permission to hedge your life. Nobody summits three mountains at once, and a career spent guarding optionality is a career spent at base camp; the view from base camp is famously not the view from the top. At the end of the day you walk one path, and the walked path gets your whole effort. Keeping a path warm costs you some evenings and the occasional favor. Walking one costs you years. Don't confuse the two prices, and don't let the cheap one talk you out of paying the expensive one.

Which is the last dial: exploration versus exploitation, the tradeoff I spent years on in Bayesian optimization. Early, with a long horizon and high uncertainty, information has time to pay itself back many times over, so you run hot: take the odd internship, sample wide, survey the range before committing to a face. Later, once you've localized a basin worth your time, you exploit: pour energy into the ascent, because a new sample can't pay itself back in the budget you have left. Warm paths are how the two coexist: you exploit with your years and explore with your evenings, sampling the range at option prices instead of restart prices. The error runs both ways: staying hot when you should cool and commit, or freezing onto one peak with decades left to look around.

## If you're a founder in the dip

You're probably not on the wrong mountain. Founders feel the descent hardest because the altitude is so legible: the round, the headcount, the graph that's supposed to only go up. When it dips, the single-mountain story screams that you're failing. Before you believe it, run the boring diagnostic. Is this a saddle, a hard stretch on a path that still reaches high ground, or a ceiling, a route whose best reachable peak is lower than you need? Look along the other axes: is the team still compounding, are you still learning fast, is there a direction that climbs even when the obvious one doesn't? If so, the job is to move through it, not torch the company. If every axis is flat and the peak is lower than you need, switching isn't quitting. It's the restart, and the camps you built come with you.

One more, from my own scars. The second time I founded a company I assumed I'd avoid my old mistakes. Instead I overfit to my old successes and turned those into the new mistakes. A dip is sometimes telling you that the map you're climbing by is last decade's map. Update the map before you decide the mountain is wrong.

## If you're a student picking a first mountain

The pressure to choose the right one is the single-mountain story arriving early, and it's mostly false. You're at the start of a long horizon with high uncertainty, the exact regime where exploration is worth the most. So your first mountain matters less for its summit than for what it teaches you about the range: what the air feels like up high, which climbs you love, which axes you actually care about. Pick something steep enough to learn on and close enough to reach.

You'll hear that switching later means falling behind. Behind whom, on which mountain? There's no shared leaderboard. I planned one dissertation and wrote another after a single summer changed what I found interesting, and that swap set up everything after it. Choosing a first mountain isn't signing a life contract. It's your first real sample of a space you'll keep learning for decades. And the range you'll spend your forties in hasn't finished forming; keep a few paths warm while it does.

None of this makes the climbing easy. The dips still hurt, the switching still costs, and you rarely see the whole surface from where you're standing. Honestly, luck does more of the work than anyone likes to admit: I'm where I am mostly because of it, plus a long trail of mistakes I slowly learned to read.

But the shape of the problem is friendlier than the story we tell about it. Many peaks, more than one of them high. Every real route dips before it rises. The map will move while you climb, which is exactly why the paths you keep warm are worth their small premiums. You can restart, and you carry your best-so-far when you do: a restart from a good base camp is nothing like starting over. There's no single summit you're failing to reach. There's always another card to turn. You're not trapped. You never were.
